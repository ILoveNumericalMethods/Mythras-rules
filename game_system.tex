\section{ACID}
\label{sec:acid}

Acids come from many different sources and are used extensively in alchemical research. All acids are  classified as either Weak, Strong or Concentrated. 

A splash or spray of acid lasts only for a few \textcolor{red}{Combat Rounds} before it loses potency. Immersion in a considerable volume of acid inflicts the damage every round until the victim or location is removed and treated.

For generic acids it is assumed that armour protects against the effects of acid but does not stop it, its Armour Points being reduced by the acid's damage until it reaches zero hit points, at which point the damage is then transferred to the hit location it protected. Armour reduced to zero Armour Points is considered useless as the acid burns through bindings, straps and joints.

\subsection{Movement}
\label{sec:movement}
Movement is broken down into three 'gaits' which are Walking, Running and Sprinting.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Walking} is the average speed a member of a particular species ambles along at when in no particular hurry. This is normally referred to as a creature's base Movement Rate.
    \item \textbf{Running} is a trot or jog, at a speed which can be maintained over long periods. Maximum running speed is triple Movement Rate, which may vary according to the bonus granted by Athletics skill \secref{sec:skills}.
    \item \textbf{Sprinting} is flat out movement at top speed, which can only be maintained for brief periods. Peak sprinting speed is five times Movement Rate, which again may be increased according to whatever bonus is granted from Athletics skill.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Moving in Armour}
\label{sec:armor-movement}
Worn armour acts against character Movement Rates, and certain kinds of actions such as swimming or climbing. The armour's \hyperref[sec:armour]{Initiative Penalty} is applied to Movement of different kinds in the following ways:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Walking:} Armour does not interfere with walking movement, although it can increase the \hyperref[sec:fatigue]{Fatigue level}.
    \item \textbf{Running or Sprinting:} Subtract the Armour Penalty from the running and sprinting speed.
    \item \textbf{Swimming:} Take the character's \textcolor{red}{swimming speed}  divide by two (rounding up), and subtract the Armour Penalty. If the result is zero, the character cannot move, and barely keeps himself afloat. If the result is negative, then the character sinks.
    \item \textbf{Climbing rough surface:} Half the Armour Penalty (rounded up) is subtracted from the base Movement Rate.
    \item \textbf{Climbing a steep surface:} The Armour Penalty is subtracted from the base Movement Rate.
    \item \textbf{Climbing a sheer surface:} Double the Armour Penalty is subtracted from the base Movement Rate.
    \item \textbf{Jumping:} Reduce the distance in metres \textcolor{red}{the character can jump}  by half the Armour Penalty (rounded up). For standing jumps this impairment is halved.
\end{itemize}


\section{AGEING}
\label{sec:ageing}
All characters age, and with age come certain consequences. The signs of ageing start at Early Middle Age (40 years for humans). As a character passes into a new Ageing Band he must make both an Endurance roll and a Willpower roll at the grades noted. If a roll is failed then he experiences Ageing Effects as shown in the Ageing Effects table.

\section{ASPHYXIATION, DROWNING AND SUFFOCATION}
\label{sec:asphyxiation}
Characters can hold their breath for a number of seconds equal to their Endurance skill. However the character must be prepared (filling the lungs with as much air as possible); if not, then the period is halved if the character was in a passive situation, or reduced to one fifth if the character was engaged in strenuous activity.

Once the period of held breath is over, characters must make an Endurance roll:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item If the roll is a critical success, no further deterioration occurs.
    \item If the roll is successful, the character accrues an extra level of \hyperref[sec:fatigue]{Fatigue}.
    \item If the roll fails, the character sustains 1d2 extra levels of \hyperref[sec:fatigue]{Fatigue} that round.
    \item If the roll is fumbled, the character sustains 1d3 extra levels of \hyperref[sec:fatigue]{Fatigue} that round.
\end{itemize}
Without aid, death from asphyxiation is usually swift. If the asphyxiation ends before the character dies, they recover Fatigue levels lost to suffocation relatively quickly; regaining one level per minute.


\section{CHARACTER IMPROVEMENT}
\label{sec:improvement}
The mechanism for character improvement is the Experience Roll. Games Masters dispense Experience Rolls at an appropriate juncture in the campaign. The number of Experience Rolls given in any one sitting should be between two and four.

\subsection{Increasing Existing Skills}
\label{sec:skill-improvement}
Any skill on the character sheet, Standard or Professional, can be increased by spending one Experience Roll.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The player rolls 1d100 and compares it to the skill being increased. The character's INT is added to the roll.
    \item If the number rolled is equal to or greater than the skill being improved it increases by 1d4 + 1\%.
    \item If the number rolled is less than the skill selected, the skill still increases but only by 1\%.
    \item If a character fumbled any skill during the course of the preceding session(s), the fumbled skill gains a free increase of 1\%.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Increasing Characteristics}
\label{sec:characteristic-improvement}
Characteristics can, like skills, be improved through Experience Rolls, which represent training regimes. Such increases are artificial boosts which normally atrophy after the training exercises cease.

To achieve and maintain characteristic increases requires that a character reduce his regular allotment of Experience Rolls by one or more points. Each Experience Roll sacrificed in this manner boosts the trained characteristic by one tenth of its rolled \textcolor{red}{species maximum}.

No matter how much training is undertaken, no characteristic can exceed its species maximum. SIZ is the exception to the above rules. It cannot be increased through mundane means.

\subsection{Increasing Passions}
\label{sec:passions-improvement}
As described under Passions, the value of a passion may be increased with Experience Rolls in exactly the same way as a skill.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

Skills can be improved without expending Experience Rolls, through help of a mentor; either a trainer or a teacher. A trainer must have at least 20\% more than the character in the skill being trained, and the degree of improvement rests on the difference.

Characters must spend one full week in training to benefit from a training increase. At the end of the training period the skill being trained improves by the die roll indicated on the Training Chart, with any modifiers due to the Teaching skill.

\subsubsection{Teachers}
\label{sec:teachers}
A teacher is a professional trainer who possesses the Teach skill, using it to improve the quality of his tuition. They utilise the same Training Chart above, but modify the amount increased according to the result of a Teach roll:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Critical Success: Skill improvement increases by two Training steps
    \item Success: Skill improvement increases by one Training step
    \item Failure: No effect on improvement
    \item Fumble: Skill improvement decreases by one Training step, which may result in the character gaining no improvement through the training
\end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Conditions:} Every toxin has one or more Conditions with specific effects.

\section{ENCUMBRANCE}
\label{sec:encumbrance}
Encumbrance represents both the mass and bulk of an item. Items that have a zero ENC value are, on their own, inconsequential; however consider that 20 zero ENC items equal 1 ENC.

\subsection{Encumbrance Capacity}
\label{sec:enc-capacity}
Characters can carry a total ENC equal to their STR × 2 with relative ease. Everyday clothing does not contribute to this capacity, but armour does.

If the total ENC borne exceeds STR × 2 then the character is considered to be \textbf{Burdened}. This has the following effects:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Skills using STR or DEX as part of their base become one grade harder
    \item Base Movement Rate drops by 2 metres, and the character can no longer sprint
    \item Carrying the load counts as \hyperref[tab:physical-effort]{Medium activity} for Fatigue purposes 
\end{itemize}

If the borne ENC exceeds STR × 3 then the character is considered to be \textbf{Overloaded}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Skills using STR or DEX as part of their base become two grades harder
    \item Base Movement Rate drops to half normal, and the character cannot move faster than a walk
    \item Carrying the load counts as \hyperref[tab:physical-effort]{Strenuous activity} for Fatigue purposes
\end{itemize}

Characters cannot carry a total ENC more than their STR × 4.

\subsection{Armour ENC}
\label{sec:armor-enc}
The amount armour counts towards Encumbrance Capacity depends on whether it is worn or carried.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item When worn, only half the total ENC of the armour is counted towards Encumbrance
    \item When carried, the full ENC value of the armour is counted towards Encumbrance
\end{itemize}

\section{HEALING}
\label{sec:healing}
Natural healing from wounds and injuries is based on the character's \textcolor{red}{Healing Rate}. The Healing Rate dictates how many Hit Points are recovered in a location depending on the injury's nature:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Minor Wounds:} The character recovers Hit Points at a rate equal to their Healing Rate \textit{per day}.
    \item \textbf{Serious Wounds:} The character recovers Hit Points at a rate equal to their Healing Rate \textit{per week}.
    \item \textbf{Major Wounds:} The character recovers Hit Points at a rate equal to their Healing Rate \textit{per month}.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Healing Restrictions}
\label{sec:healing-restrictions}
There are certain restrictions on natural healing:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The healing character cannot engage in strenuous activity: otherwise the Healing Rate is reduced by 1d3. Thus, a character recovering from even a Minor Wound could find his progress halted if he decides to engage any physical tasks that might exacerbate his injuries.
    \item Natural healing will not begin to heal a Major Wound until that location has been treated with a successful Healing roll.
    \item Non-dismembering Major Wounds which are not treated with successful Healing roll within a number of days equal to one twentieth of the Healing skill become maimed, permanently reducing the Hit Points of the location.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Magical Healing}
\label{sec:magic-healing}
Some magic can heal the wounds suffered by a victim. Yet there are specific restrictions as to what level of wound can be treated by each spell. 

No matter how petty the healing spell or miracle, its application is always enough to stabilise any type of wound, preventing bleeding and immediate death even if it doesn't actually cure the underlying injury. Note that this only applies to gross physical trauma, not to conditions brought about by suffocation, poison, and the like.

\subsection{Permanent Injuries}
\label{sec:permanent-injuries}

\section{LUCK POINTS}
\label{sec:luck}
Luck Points help differentiate heroes from the rank and file. Every character starts with a number of Luck Points. Luck Points can be used during play and, at the beginning of the next session, replenish to their usual value.

\subsection{Using Luck Points}
Only one Luck Point can be used in support of a particular action.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cheat Fate:} Re-roll any dice roll that affects them. Force an opponent to re-roll.
    \item \textbf{Desperate Effort:} Gain an additional \textcolor{red}{Action Point} if exhausted.
    \item \textbf{Mitigate Damage:} Suffering a Major \textcolor{red}{Wound} may spend a Luck Point to downgrade the injury to a Serious Wound.
\end{itemize}


\section{PASSIONS}
\label{sec:passions}
A Passion is any deeply held commitment that has the capacity to influence events during play.

\subsection{Using Passions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item To augment another skill, reflecting the depth of one's feeling. Adds 20\% of its value to a skill (if allowed).
    \item As an ability in its own right to drive choices. A standard roll is made against a Passion.
    \item To oppose other Passions - even those held by the same character. This is typically used where two Passions would conflict.
    \item As a general measure of depth of commitment.
    \item To resist psychological manipulation or magical domination, substituting for Willpower (if allowed).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deepening and Waning}
Passions can increase during a game independently of \hyperref[sec:improvement]{Experience roll} , based on the strength of whatever occurred to trigger the increase. Passions can also wane.

\section{SURVIVAL}
\label{sec:survival}
Characters may find themselves in hostile environments battling the elements: exposure, starvation, and thirst.

Each danger has a specific onset time:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Exposure:} Assuming suitable clothing it usually starts to take effect after CON hours.
    \item \textbf{Starvation:} Critical levels begin after a number of days equal to half CON.
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} Begins after CON x4 hours.
\end{itemize}

Once conditions take hold characters start to accrue \hyperref[sec:fatigue]{Fatigue} levels. An Endurance roll is required at onset, which is then repeated every time the Exposure, Starvation or Dehydration Rate cycles.

\section{Bartering and Haggling}
\label{sec:trade}

\subsection{Bartering}
\label{sec:bartering}

Bartering depends on offering something the other party needs or finds valuable in their normal environment.

\textbf{Process:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Character makes an offer of something.
    \item If offering a common commodity or essential service obviously beneficial to the other party, the barter succeeds automatically.
    \item If offering something of less obvious value/utility, make a Customs or relevant Culture roll.
    \item Make an Opposed Influence or Commerce roll. The other party resists with Willpower or Commerce (whichever is higher).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Melee Weapons}
\label{sec:melee-weapons}

Melee weapons are governed by a series of characteristics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Damage:} Amount of damage delivered on successful attack
    \item \textbf{Size:} Ability to inflict and \textcolor{red}{parry} damage (S, M, L, H, E)
    \item \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Reach}:} Length and ability to hold a foe at bay (T, S, M, L, VL)
    \item \textbf{Combat Effects:} Access to \textcolor{red}{special effects} 
    \item \textbf{ENC:} Combined encumbrance and weight
    \item \textbf{AP/HP:} Armour Points and Hit Points
    \item \textbf{Traits:} Special characteristics (see weapon-traits below)
    \item \textbf{Cost:} In Silver Pieces
    \item \textbf{Milieu:} Typical era (P - Stone Age level, A - Bronze and Iron Ages, M - European Middle Ages, R - European Renaissance, E - 17th-18th Centuries, I - Industrial Revolution)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Weapon Traits}
\label{sec:weapon-traits}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Barbed:} If \textcolor{red}{impaled}, will cause full damage when yanked free rather than half
    \item \textbf{Defensive:} If used to \textcolor{red}{parry} an attacking weapon, that weapon is considered one size smaller
    \item \textbf{Double-Ended:} Length remaining in hand can be reversed and used as a shorter version
    \item \textbf{Entrapping:} Allows use of the \textcolor{red}{Pin} Weapon special effect without needing to roll a Critical
    \item \textbf{Flexible:} Shield is considered one size smaller when parrying
    \item \textbf{Mounted:} Must be used from the back of a moving mount
    \item \textbf{Offensive:} Cannot be used to \textcolor{red}{parry}
    \item \textbf{Passive Block:} Can \textcolor{red}{passively block} multiple Hit Locations, must be continuous to another
    \item \textbf{Ranged:} Can parry ranged weapons
    \item \textbf{Set:} Can be used to receive a foot or mounted charge
    \item \textbf{Stealth:} Must be used from ambush
    \item \textbf{Thrown:} Can be used as a ranged weapon as well as hand-to-hand
\end{itemize}

\section{Ranged Weapons}
\label{sec:ranged-weapons}


Ranged weapons have very similar characteristics to Melee Weapons, although there are some differences as noted below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Damage:} Damage dealt on successful attack.
    \item \textbf{Damage Modifier:} Yes/No - whether user's damage modifier applies.
    \item \textbf{Force:} Impact rating (S/M/L/H/E) determining ability to overcome shields.
    \item \textbf{Range:} Three range bands in metres: Close/Effective/Long.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Close Range:} Choose Location special effect allowed against stationary or unaware targets without requiring a Critical.
        \item \textbf{Effective Range:} No significant modifiers on accuracy or power.
        \item \textbf{Long Range:} Damage halved, Force reduced by one step.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Load:} Number of turns required to load or reload ammunition.
    \item \textbf{Combat Effects:} Same \textcolor{red}{special effects} as close combat weapons.
    \item \textbf{Impaling Size:} Effective size (S/M/L/H/E) of weapon or ammunition for impaling attacks.
    \item \textbf{ENC:} Combined encumbrance and weight.
    \item \textbf{AP/HP:} Armour Points and Hit Points - damage resistance and durability.
    \item \textbf{Cost:} Price in Silver Pieces.
    \item \textbf{Milieu:} Typical era (P - Stone Age level, A - Bronze and Iron Ages, M - European Middle Ages, R - European Renaissance, E - 17th-18th Centuries, I - Industrial Revolution)
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Critical Success: +2 Enhancements
    \item Success: +1 Enhancement
    \item Failure: No Enhancements
    \item Fumble: Reduce Quality by 25\%

\section{Skills Overview}

Skills are divided into two categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard Skills}: Common to everyone, representing innate abilities employed regularly from any walk of life.
    \item \textbf{Professional Skills}: Specialized training that differs between characters, reflecting expertise developed within a career and culture.
\end{itemize}

Despite these distinctions, Standard and Professional Skills work identically in play.

\section{How Skills Work}

A skill has a base value calculated from characteristics (either two characteristics added together, or one characteristic multiplied by two). There is no upper limit to a skill's value; it can exceed 100\%.

\subsection{Skill Resolution}

Whenever a character attempts a test or challenge, roll 1d100 and compare to the skill value:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Success}: Roll equal to or less than the skill value
    \item \textbf{Failure}: Roll greater than the skill value
    \item Any roll of 01-05 is always a success
    \item Any roll of 96-00 is always a failure, regardless of skill value
    \item A Critical success occurs on a roll equal to or less than one-tenth of the skill's value (round fractions up).
    \item Skills 100\% or less: Fumble on 99 or 00
    \item Skills above 100\%: Fumble only on 00
\end{itemize}

\section{Special Skill Situations}

\subsection{Reattempting Skills}
\label{sec:reattempt}

After a failed skill attempt, a last-ditch follow-up attempt may be permitted but:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty increases by one grade
    \item If this second effort fails, no further attempts possible
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Augmenting Skills}
\label{sec:augment}

One skill can aid another if appropriate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item GM decides if augmentation is appropriate
    \item Can only augment primary skill with one other skill
    \item Augmenting skill adds twice its critical range (20\% of skill) to primary skill
    \item Critical and Fumble chances remain based on unaugmented primary skill
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Capping Skills}
\label{sec:cap}

Primary skill is restricted to value of secondary skill when the latter is lower, typically when knowledge is limited by unusual circumstances.

\section{Contested Rolls}
If the highest-skilled participant has skill exceeding 100\%, subtract the difference between 100\% and their skill value from everyone in the contest, including themselves.


\subsection{Opposed Rolls}
\label{sec:opposed}

Used where challenge results in win/lose, pass/fail:
If the highest-skilled participant has skill exceeding 100\%, subtract the difference between 100\% and their skill value from everyone in the contest, including themselves.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Both participants roll respective skills
    \item Winner is the one with better level of success
    \item If same level of success, winner is the one with highest roll within success range
\end{enumerate}

If both participants fail in an Opposed Roll then either:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Describe the situation, leaving both sides hanging in dramatic tension, then re-roll the contest to establish a winner.
    \item The Games Master should produce some explanation or circumstances that suit the nature of the failure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Group Rolls}
\label{sec:group}

\textbf{Team Roll}
Success is cooperative. Roll once for highest or lowest skill; success means everyone succeeds, failure means everyone fails.

\textbf{Sorting Roll}
Success is individual. Make one roll for the group; those with skill above the roll succeed, those below fail.

\textbf{Proportional Skill Success}
For large groups performing same task with no margin for error, apply skill as percentage of group success.


\section{Athletics (STR+DEX)}
\label{skill:athletics}

Covers climbing, jumping, running, and throwing. Armour affects these activities based on \hyperref[sec:armor-movement]{Armour Penalty}.

\subsection{Climbing}
\label{skill:climbing}


\textbf{Critical}: Scale extremely quickly or avoid hidden danger\\
\textbf{Failure}: Climb aborted\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Fall (cannot use Acrobatics to reduce damage)

\subsection{Jumping}
\label{skill:jumping}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{With run-up (5m minimum)}: Up to twice height horizontally, or half height vertically
    \item \textbf{Standing jump}: Halve above distances
    \item \textbf{Armour}: Reduce jump distance in metres by half Armour Penalty (min 1m)
    \item \textbf{Athletics bonus}: +1m horizontal or +20cm vertical per full 20\%
\end{itemize}

Jumping over half maximum distance results in falling prone on landing.

\textbf{Critical}: +1m to distance, remain upright\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Land awkwardly → Endurance roll: success = 1 damage to leg, failure = 1d4 damage

\subsection{Running}
\label{skill:running}

+1m to base Movement when sprinting per full 25\%, or half that for long distances

\textbf{Critical}: +1m to Movement rate\\
\textbf{Failure}: Increase fatigue level\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Endurance roll: success = 1 damage to leg, failure = 1d4 damage

\subsection{Throwing}
\label{skill:throwing}

For hurling large/unwieldy objects. Maximum range = 1m per point STR exceeds object's SIZ.

Weapons thrown point-first \textcolor{red}{Combat Style}.

\section{Boating (STR+CON)}
\label{skill:boating}

Operation of small craft on rivers, lakes, inshore. For large \hyperref[sec:vehicles]{vessels} vessels .

\textbf{Critical}: +1/10 to vessel's Movement rate\\
\textbf{Failure}: Movement halved or destination missed\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Lose oar, take on water, or capsize in bad conditions

\section{Brawn (STR+SIZ)}
\label{skill:brawn}

Applying row phisical force: lifting, breaking, and so on.

\subsection{Lifting}
\label{skill:lifting}

Base capacity = STR in SIZ (1 SIZ ≈ 5kg). +1 SIZ per full 10\% skill.

Maximum brief lift = 2 × STR + skill bonus.

\textbf{Failure}: Endurance roll or 1d3 damage to random location (head excluded)\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Automatic injury

\subsection{Breaking}
\label{skill:breaking}

Use maximum lifting capacity with \textcolor{red}{Damage Modifier} table to determine damage per attempt.

\subsection{Contests of Strength}
\label{skill:strength-contest}

Simple opposed roll. If \textcolor{red}{Damage Modifier} differs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 1 step difference - Hard difficulty for smaller
    \item 2 steps difference - Formidable difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section{Conceal (DEX+POW)}
\label{skill:conceal}

Hiding objects rather than self. Counterpart to \hyperref[skill:stealth]{Stealth}.

\textbf{Critical}: Discovery rolls one grade harder\\
\textbf{Failure}: Automatically found if searched\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Object falls/rolls into open, drawing attention

\section{Customs (INT×2)}
\label{skill:customs}

Knowledge of own community's social codes, rites, taboos.

\textbf{Critical}: Exemplary action or subtle nuance detected\\
\textbf{Failure}: Minor irritation or humor\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Offence given (rebuke to violent punishment)

\section{Dance (DEX+CHA)}
\label{skill:dance}

\textbf{Critical}: Expressive, fluid performance; as persuasive as \hyperref[skill:influence]{Influence}, or subsequent Influence gets bonus equal to critical score\\
\textbf{Failure}: Lackluster performance\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Trip or stumble; communication skills penalized

\section{Deceit (INT+CHA)}
\label{skill:deceit}

Masking truth, lying, bluffing, hiding emotions. Opposes \hyperref[skill:insight]{Insight}.

\textbf{Critical}: Convincing deceit; truth won't be questioned unless exposed\\
\textbf{Failure}: Lacked conviction, prompting investigation\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Transparent failure; future credibility damaged

\section{Drive (DEX+POW)}
\label{skill:drive}

Control of wheeled/drawn vehicles.

\textbf{Critical}: +1/10 Movement or skillful feat\\
\textbf{Failure}: Halve Movement\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Breakdown or \hyperref[sec:falling]{overturn} (occupants need \hyperref[skill:acrobatics]{Acrobatics} or \hyperref[skill:evade]{Evade} to avoid damage)

\section{Endurance (CON×2)}
\label{skill:endurance}

Physical stress, pain, fatigue resistance. Optional limit: Cannot exceed CON×5.

\textbf{Critical}: Shrug off worst effects; act when incapacitated\\
\textbf{Failure}: Succumb to stress/injury\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Overwhelmed; incapacitated; auto-fail further resistance

\section{Evade (DEX×2)}
\label{skill:evade}

Escape danger, avoid missiles, traps, spells.

\textbf{Opposed by}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textcolor{red}{Combat Style} (missiles)
    \item Trap's Potency or designer's skill (traps)
    \item Caster's casting skill (spells)
    \item Attacker's Evade (combat maneuvers)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Critical}: Retain advantage/composure\\
\textbf{Fumble}: character has left himself wide open to the hazard, for instance: maximum damage from a trap or ending up prone.

The effect of Criticals and Fumbles during combat is a \textcolor{red}{special case}.

\section{First Aid (DEX+INT)}
\label{skill:firstaid}

Treat \textcolor{red}{injuries}. Takes 1d3 minutes. Requires bandages/equipment. Once per specific injury. If multiple body parts was damageed, can be applyed to each part.

\begin{longtable}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Injury} & \textbf{Successful Treatment} \\
\hline
Asphyxiated & Victim breathes again \\
Bleeding & Blood flow staunched \\
Impaled & Item removed without further damage \\
Unconsciousness & Restored to awareness \\
Minor Wound & Restores 1d3 hit points \\
Serious Wound & Restores partial functionality \\
Major Wound & Stabilizes area, prevents death \\
\hline
\end{longtable}

\textbf{Critical}: Minor = 1d6 HP, Serious = 1d3 HP, Major = partial functionality\\
\textbf{Fumble}: +1 damage, potentially increasing severity

\section{Influence (CHA×2)}
\label{skill:influence}

Persuasion, bribery. Opposed by \hyperref[skill:perception]{Perception}, \hyperref[skill:willpower]{Willpower}, or Influence.

\textbf{Critical}: Next Influence test with same person one grade easier\\
\textbf{Failure}: Refusal, no issues\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Offence given; further attempts one grade harder

Reading verbal non-verbal behaviour, opposes \hyperref[skill:deceit]{Deceit}.

\textbf{Critical}: In-depth understanding; subsequent Deceit/Influence one grade easier\\
\textbf{Failure}: Nothing discerned\\
\textbf{Fumble}: Completely misjudge; Deceit/Influence one grade harder

Knowledge of local flora, fauna, terrain, weather.

\section{Native Tongue (INT+CHA)}
\label{skill:nativetongue}

The ability to speak one's own language learned growing up in one's home culture. Measures articulation, eloquence, and depth of vocabulary. Unlike other skills, Native Tongue is not rolled against directly. It serves as a static representation of overall fluency, limiting the level of conversational interaction.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dialect Understanding}: Helps understand dialects of neighboring peoples sharing common linguistic origin. The skill is treated at least one grade harder in such cases, potentially affecting fluency.
    \item \textbf{Verbal Skill Cap}: Acts as a cap on verbal skills requiring good language grasp (see \hyperref[sec:cap]{Capping Skills}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Careers}
\label{sec:intro}

A character’s career is the profession they have trained in, which defines the initial scope of their skills. The choice of career dictates how starting skill points are allocated and which professional skills are available. The careers listed in this chapter are available to most cultural backgrounds, but the final selection should be agreed upon with the Games Master.

\subsection{Career Progression}
\label{subsec:progression}

Characters develop their career skills in the following way:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Selection:} Choose up to three skills from the \hyperref[sec:skill_table]{Professional Skills} list available to that career.
    \item \textbf{Distribution:} Distribute 100 points among the career's \hyperref[sec:skill_table]{Standard Skills} and the chosen professional skills. Each skill can be increased by up to +15 points from this pool. Skills start at their base value.
    \item \textbf{Combat Styles:} Newly chosen \textcolor{red}{Combat Style} are treated as Standard Skills and start at a base value equal to the character's (STR+DEX) or as defined by the \textcolor{red}{culture}.
\end{enumerate}
At this stage, characters also gain a pool of bonus skill points based on their \hyperref[sec:ageing]{Age} to further customize their abilities.

\subsection{Careers and Social Class}
\label{subsec:social-class}

There is no restrictions on what careers are available to what social class, but it is something players and Games Masters may wish to consider. Some social classes will look down on members who practice certain vocations. Games Masters should decide if a career is denied to a particular social class, as this can create interesting roleplaying opportunities.
For full descriptions see \hyperref[sec:all_careers]{list of all Careers}

\subsection*{Agent}
\label{career:agent}
\career{Agent} -- Agitator, Assassin, Detective, Informer, Spy.\\

\section{Creating a Custom Career}
\label{sec:custom-career}

Players can create custom careers subject to the following guidelines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Agree the career with your Games Master. Your concept of Death Knight Assassin might not fit with the themes and style of the world.
    \item \textbf{Standard Skills:} Choose up to seven \hyperref[sec:skill_table]{Standard Skills} and seven \hyperref[sec:skill_table]{Professional Skills} for the profession, reflecting its nature. Remember that magical abilities are considered Professional Skills.
    \item Also remember that \hyperref[sec:combat-styles]{Combat Styles} are treated as Standard Skills. Try to avoid granting Combat Styles except to those careers where physical conflict is a regular occurrence or fundamental to survival.
\end{itemize}

\section{Culture and Skill Points}
\label{sec:culture}

Culture defines the society in which the character was raised, determining their worldview, skills, and passions. There are four basic human cultures: Barbarian, Civilised, Nomadic, and Primitive.

Each culture includes a description, Standard Skills, example Combat Styles, Professional Skills, and Cultural Passions. Apply skill bonuses immediately to the character sheet.

When applying cultural skill points:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply static bonuses to Customs and Native Tongue (both 40\%).
    \item Select three Professional Skills from those offered.
    \item Distribute points among Standard Skills, chosen Professional Skills, and a Combat Style (1\% per point spent).
    \item Maximum +15 points per skill, minimum +5 points per skill.
\end{enumerate}

Professional Skills cannot be attempted without years of study. Characters have zero ability in any Professional Skill unless specifically chosen. Combat Styles are treated as Standard Skills starting at (STR+DEX).

\section{Barbarian Culture}
\label{sec:barbarian}

Tribal in nature, barbarians tend to shun civilisation, viewing those who live in large towns and cities as weak and corrupt. Although their own settlements are also sedentary, they tend to be far smaller and closer to nature. Barbarian tribes occupy distinct territories, often half-tamed wilderness, which they regard as their own for hunting, grazing, and farming purposes. Most are adept in the use of weaponry as they must overcome many dangers throughout their lives – facing creatures of the wilds or mustering to defend their lands against rival tribes. Since they live in small communities, the ties of kinship and clan are strong elements of their society.


\subsection{Skills}
\label{sec:barbarian-skills}

\textbf{Standard Skills:} Athletics, Brawn, Endurance, First Aid, Locale, Perception; and either Boating or Ride.

\textbf{Example Combat Styles:} Barbarian Fyrdman, Berserker, Horse Eater, Seaborne Revier, Weapon Thegn, Wolf Hunter.

\textbf{Professional Skills:} Craft (any), Healing, Lore (any), Musicianship, Navigate, Seamanship, Survival, Track.

\subsection{Cultural Passions}
\label{sec:barbarian-passions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Loyalty to Clan Chieftain
    \item Love (friend, sibling or romantic lover)
    \item Hate (creature, rival or clan)
\end{itemize}

\section{Civilised Culture}
\label{sec:civilised}

The Civilised culture is epitomised by a semblance of law and order, underpinned by complex social codes, and supported by a bureaucracy. Civilised people believe themselves superior to all other cultures because their achievements tend towards permanence: sprawling cities, imposing temples, celebratory monuments, and the recording of history and events in books, tomes, and scrolls. Of course, this is a thin veneer. The most civilised of cities can be more lawless than any barbarian frontier settlement; and the social codes may be cruder than the hospitality rituals of nomads. But the Civilised culture prides itself on its achievements, its infrastructure, its professional artisans, and its ability to easily extend its reach to wherever it wishes to go.

\section{Nomadic Culture}
\label{sec:nomadic}

Nomadic people are constantly on the move, with no home and hearth to call their own. They may wander aimlessly about, or might have several camps they move to and from throughout the year. They raise few crops and instead follow the migrations of animals or fish, perhaps even herding their own domesticated beasts. Nomads are adept at subsisting on what they can quickly and easily scavenge from their surroundings, letting nothing go to waste. The skills of the nomad culture should be tailored to their environment. Some journey vast distances on foot, others live in caravans or ride strange creatures, and some drift across the oceans on great floating rafts.


\subsection{Skills}
\label{sec:nomadic-skills}

\textbf{Standard Skills:} Endurance, First Aid, Locale, Perception, Stealth; and two of: Athletics, Boating, Swim, Drive or Ride.

\textbf{Example Combat Styles:} Camel Cavalry, Feathered Death Flinger, Horse Lord, Whale Hunter, Wheeled Warrior, Wolf Runner.

\section{Primitive Culture}
\label{sec:primitive}

Of all peoples, primitives are those that live closest with the land, in tune with its hidden secrets. Other cultures commonly dismiss them as little more than animals, yet they merely eschew the technology that so many others take for granted; relying instead on flint spears and arrows, and their skill for survival in the wild. Living in extended family groups, primitive cultures gather around very simple dwellings that can range from caves and areas of natural shelter through to lean-tos or very basic huts. Primitives are typically hunter-gatherers with either few, or poorly developed, systems for farming and husbandry. However, certain cultural beliefs, often based on superstition, can be very well defined. Few primitive cultures have developed anything approaching a written language although paintings and other pictorial symbols serve as a means of communication.


\subsection{Skills}
\label{sec:primitive-skills}

\section{Allies, Contacts, Rivals and Enemies}
\label{sec:personal-connections}

Allies, Contacts, Rivals, and Enemies are people, or perhaps organisations, that a character has developed a relationship with for either good or ill. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ally:} a friend, colleague, or relation who shares the same cause as the character and will offer help and guidance when called upon. Depending on their personality, the Ally may want a favour in return or might provide aid for free.
    \item \textbf{Contact:} people of potential influence the character knows. A Contact is an acquaintance who can help, not one who will.
    \item \textbf{Rival:} those who oppose the character for the same or similar goals, and will attempt to interfere with or harass him.
    \item \textbf{Enemy:} someone who wishes the character harm, and will go to lengths to ensure the character suffers in some way. Enemies may take direct or indirect action, possibly trying to influence contacts and allies to bring about the character's misfortune.
\end{itemize}

Allies, Contacts, Rivals, and Enemies are intended to act in the background, providing occasional interactions with the character but not always being prominent in their lives. These can be good sources of background information and, in certain circumstances, drive crucial parts of a scenario, but they are generally intended to be bit-players in the characters' stories rather than fully-fleshed non-player characters. Of course, they may well develop into fully-formed non-player characters as a campaign develops, and so Games Masters should keep careful note of who they are, how they look and act, what they know, and how they have (and will) interact with the character. This will maintain consistency in how they are portrayed.

\subsection{Personal Connections}
\label{sec:personal-connections}

Why are the characters adventuring together? What connects them and forms the companionship? It is recommended that each player choose at least one of his fellows and, working with the chosen colleague, devise a reason for the relationship. Some examples:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Childhood friends who have continued together into adulthood.
    \item Members of the same community or culture.
    \item Members of the same profession, or if from different professions, have had chance to meet and do business.
    \item A lucky encounter in a tavern, inn, or other social establishment.
    \item A shared acquaintance (such as a third character in the group).
\end{itemize}

Starting characters wishing to form a personal connection as part of their background are rewarded with a pool of Luck Points available to the entire party, rather than just a single individual. The number of \textcolor{red}{Luck Points} starts at two and increases by 1 per character who has formed a personal connection within the group. This pool of points functions in a very similar way to the individual Luck Points a character has, but are there for the benefit of all those within the group. They can only be drawn upon by a character when he is actively helping, or intends to help, another character (this can be anyone in the group needing help; not just the character with whom a relationship has been established).


\section{Passions}
\label{sec:passions-detailed}
Passions are measured in the same way as skills; they have a starting value based on two characteristics and are rated between 1 and 100 (or higher). Passions represent several things:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Loyalties and allegiances
    \item Strongly held beliefs or ideals
    \item Emotion felt towards someone or something
\end{itemize}

A Passion can be improved in the same way as a skill, or it can be either increased or decreased by the Games Master depending on circumstances.

A Passion can be for anything – a person, an organisation, an ideal, even an object. Passions are typically described by a verb such as: Comfort, Desire, Despise, Destroy, Espouse, Fear, Flee, Foreswear, Hate, Love, Loyalty to, Protect, Repudiate, Respect, Seek, Subvert, Torment, or Uphold.

The verb describes the nature of the relationship. Loyalty, for instance, does not always indicate respect; Respect does not always indicate love; Despising something is not the same as hating it; Loving someone is not the same as being loyal to them. The verb used should therefore reflect how the character feels either personally or culturally towards the subject.

\subsection{Marriage}
\label{sec:marriage}

This is purely optional, but is the character married or betrothed to be married? Marriage is the most common way for families to advance their status, position, and power, and so making marriage arrangements, even for children who are not yet old enough to be married, is common.

To determine Marital Status, roll 1d100 for the character and compare it with the character's base value \textcolor{red}{Influence skill}. If the result is equal to or less than base value Influence, then the character is Betrothed – that is, has been promised into a marriage. If the 1d100 roll is equal to or less than 10\% of the character's Influence, then the character is already married.

How happy or prosperous this marriage is depends entirely on the player and Games Master. Take a little time to think about the nature of the relationship: is it a political or economic betrothal? Does Social Class play a part? Do the two partners love each other? Perhaps they hate each other. If love or hate are involved, then consider establishing a Passion to represent the depth and nature of the relationship.

If the character is married, have any children formed from the union? Again, this can be an agreed situation between the player and Games Master. Or the player can roll 1d4-1 to determine the number of children present. If there are children, then roll 1d4 to determine the ages of the children. If the age rolls are identical, then the character has produced twins or even triplets.
